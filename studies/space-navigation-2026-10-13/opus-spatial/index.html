1 · Flanks — the screen edges hold the count

The left and right edges each show one tick per desktop that lies in that direction. The tick nearest the content is the adjacent desktop, drawn longer and brighter. On a switch, a tick moves from one edge to the other, and a cyan wash with the new number pulses in from the edge you entered through.

  • Boundary: on the last desktop the right edge is empty. Absence says "nothing further" without reacting to the extra press.
  • Rapid / reversal: counts are state, not animation. They are always correct at any rate; the wash just restarts.
  • Reduce Motion: no pulse travel; the wash holds briefly and fades.
  • Why: peripheral vision catches edge change without pulling your eyes to the top of the screen. Nothing covers the work area.

2 · Landing — feedback where your attention lands

The focused window on the new desktop gets a thin outline and a small tag: the desktop number, the app and an arrow for the direction of travel. When the tray is already up, the outline morphs from the previous desktop's focused window to the new one. The movement across the screen makes the jump feel spatial.

  • Boundary: silent. The outline stays on the current window until it fades.
  • Rapid / reversal: the outline retargets mid-morph; one element, never a queue.
  • Reduce Motion: no morph; the outline appears in place and fades.
  • Why: after a switch the question is usually "where is my cursor or focus?", not "which number am I on?". This answers both in one place.

3 · Ghost carry — outlines give the switch direction

The real switch is instant. Outlines of the windows you just left slide off toward where you came from and fade. Along both screen edges, the neighbouring desktops peek in as wireframe slabs: their screen edge, their windows and their number. The slab in the direction of travel is brighter and settles in.

  • Boundary: on the first or last desktop no slab peeks from that side, so the edge of the set is visible before you press.
  • Rapid / reversal: only the newest departure keeps a ghost (older ghosts are cut). Reversal slides the ghost the other way.
  • Reduce Motion: ghosts fade in place; slabs appear without sliding.
  • Why: it is the closest thing to a camera move that needs no pixels. You see the shape of the next and previous desktops, not a number.

Would need screenshots or private compositing

  • A real carousel or parallax of window contents (pixels of other desktops). Needs ScreenCaptureKit captures per space, which can be stale and memory-heavy. Rejected.
  • Frosted or blurred backdrops under the overlay. Rejected for cost and identity.
  • Delaying the system switch to run our own transition. Violates "never delay". Rejected.

Preferred direction

Ghost carry, built in two steps. Step 1 ships only the departing ghost and needs nothing new: frames come from the on-screen list read just before the switch. That alone turns an instant cut into a felt lateral move. Step 2 adds the neighbour slabs once Lattices' window index exposes per-space frames (the Spaces strip already draws shapes from a WindowServer read). Landing is the best Reduce Motion fallback and the quickest win. Flanks is the quietest option and could replace the top rail completely for people who find any center-screen HUD noisy.

Limitations. Browser simulation: CSS and WAAPI timing stand in for Core Animation. Window layouts are invented. Multi-display, full-screen apps and Stage Manager are not modelled. Panel cost on ProMotion displays and the latency of reading per-space frames are untested.
